Plants, animals, and sky 21

What lived and grew here, and the sky, in these years21
  1. to 1900
    Passenger pigeon. Wintered across the South in huge flocks and roosts. Extinct in the wild by the early 1900s; the last known bird died at the Cincinnati Zoo, 1 Sep 1914. An Alabama or Jefferson County account is still to find.Then and now: land, sky and wildlife, section 7 (to be sourced)General history
  2. to 1903
    American chestnut. Ranged from Maine to Alabama and bore heavy nut crops every year, unlike oaks, which boom and bust. Chestnut and chestnut oak are among the trees the 1821 surveyors named most often on the home sections.Then and now: land, sky and wildlife, sections 9 and 13; Penn State Extension; WorldAtlasDocumented
  3. to 1909
    Black bear. Historically native throughout Alabama; nearly extirpated in the early 1900s.Then and now: land, sky and wildlife, section 15; National Park Service, Little River CanyonDocumented
  4. to 1918
    Carolina parakeet. America's only native parrot. Audubon warned in 1831 that where they had been plentiful scarcely any were to be seen. The last captive bird, Incas, died at the Cincinnati Zoo on 21 Feb 1918. Whether parakeets reached the Jefferson County uplands: Unresolved.Then and now: land, sky and wildlife, section 17; Smithsonian; Kevin Burgio, University of ConnecticutDocumented
  5. to 1922
    Red wolf. Once lived in a variety of habitats statewide, in small groups; also took sheep, goats, pigs, and sometimes calves. Reported near extinction in Alabama in 1921 and gone by the early 1920s.Outdoor Alabama, Red wolfDocumented
  6. to 1924
    White-tailed deer: the decline. Alabama had a thriving deer population before and during the colonial period; unregulated hunting, market hunting and habitat loss then pushed them down. In 1908 deer were present in 38 of the state's 67 counties, "only a few thousand"; by 1922, in only 11. Estimates of the low differ (about 2,000 or about 10,000): Unresolved.Then and now: land, sky and wildlife, section 10; AP, 2017, citing Davis, The White-Tailed Deer in Alabama (1979); Outdoor Alabama; Discovering AlabamaDocumented
  7. to 1939
    Bald eagle. Present in Alabama in William's time (General history). Killed by people, and later harmed by pesticides, in the early 1900s; killing them was not outlawed until the Bald Eagle Protection Act of 1940.Alabama Ornithological Society; Alabama TodayGeneral history
  8. to 1940
    Mosquitoes and malaria. Malaria was endemic across large parts of the South until the 1930s. A study of Alabama's 67 counties found that drainage projects (much of it WPA work) and new county health departments brought it down. The federal agency that became the CDC was created during World War II to fight Southern malaria. Malaria in Jefferson County and in the family: Unresolved.Then and now: land, sky and wildlife, section 22; Sledge and Mohler, American Journal of Public Health, 2013; NPR, 2013Documented
  9. to 1942
    Wild turkey. Habitat loss, unregulated hunting and market hunting brought the wild turkey to the edge of extinction in Alabama; about 10,000 were thought to remain in the early 1900s. A somewhat isolated population held on around the Bankhead National Forest.Then and now: land, sky and wildlife, section 11; Alabama Living (retired ADCNR biologist Steve Barnett); Outdoor AlabamaDocumented
  10. to 2026
    Chuck-will's-widow. A native summer resident that nests on the ground in open woods (General history, to be sourced).Then and now: land, sky and wildlife, section 6General history
  11. 1829 to 2011
    Periodical cicadas: Brood XIX years. Counting back by 13 years from 2024, Brood XIX's emergence years are 2011, 1998, 1985, 1972, 1959, 1946, 1933, 1920, 1907, 1894, 1881, 1868, 1855, 1842 and 1829. Whether the brood emerges at Mount Olive: Unresolved.Then and now: land, sky and wildlife, section 21Computed
  12. 1890 to 1949
    Beaver: trapped out. Extremely scarce in Alabama by 1890. Only 41 beaver dams were found in the state in 1929, and the population in the 1930s was about 500 animals. Restocking began in the 1940s.Then and now: land, sky and wildlife, section 12; Hill, Vertebrate Pest Conference; Alabama Cooperative ExtensionDocumented
  13. 1890 to 2026
    European starling and house sparrow. The starling was released in Central Park, New York, in 1890 by the American Acclimatization Society, which also introduced the house sparrow. Their spread helped bring about the Lacey Act of 1900. "The Starling in Montgomery, Alabama" was published in The Auk in 1918. When either reached Jefferson County: Unresolved.Then and now: land, sky and wildlife, section 18; NPS, Chattahoochee; Carolina Bird Club; SORA (Auk 1918)Documented
  14. 1900
    Total solar eclipse, 28 May 1900. The path of totality ran from New Orleans through south Alabama to Norfolk (NOAA). About 96% of the Sun covered at Mount Olive, about 7:50 am local sun time (worked out with PyEphem; not yet checked against NASA).Then and now: land, sky and wildlife, section 28; NOAA NCEIComputed
  15. 1900 to 2026
    Coyote. Originally a western species that moved east. Some were released in Alabama as game animals in the early 1900s, after the wolves disappeared.Alabama Living, Coyotes (quoting ADCNR biologist Richard Tharp)Documented
  16. 1904 to 1929
    Chestnut blight. The blight fungus was found killing chestnuts at the Bronx Zoo in 1904. It reached North Carolina by 1914 and was fully entrenched in the Deep South by 1929. Trees usually died within two years of the blight reaching them. Some American chestnuts still survive in Alabama; a healthy 85-foot tree was found in the Talladega National Forest.Then and now: land, sky and wildlife, section 13; Alabama Heritage; Penn State Extension; American Chestnut FoundationDocumented
  17. 1910
    Halley's Comet, May 1910. Earth passed through the comet's tail. A scientist's warning picked up by newspapers set off a panic, and "comet pills" and gas masks were sold to frightened buyers.Then and now: land, sky and wildlife, section 28; Science History InstituteDocumented
  18. 1910 to 1979
    Black bear: gone from most of the state. For several generations the only bears left in Alabama were around the Mobile River basin. When they were gone from Jefferson County is not known.Then and now: land, sky and wildlife, section 15; National Park Service, Little River CanyonUnresolved
  19. 1910 to 2003
    Boll weevil. Reached Mobile County in 1910 and the whole state by 1916; losses in the first years ran 60 to 75%. Enterprise dedicated its Boll Weevil Monument in 1919. No economic damage in Alabama since 1995; the last weevil was caught in Mobile County in 2003. Whether Nin and Bud grew cotton, and how much: Unresolved.Then and now: land, sky and wildlife, section 20; Encyclopedia of AlabamaDocumented
  20. 1916
    Elk brought back, 1916. Around 1916 the state tried to bring back elk with 55 animals from Yellowstone; the attempt did not last. Weak sources; to verify against ADCNR.Then and now: land, sky and wildlife, section 16; Alabama Pioneers; Biology InsightsDocumented
  21. 1918 to 2026
    Imported fire ants. The black imported fire ant came into the port of Mobile about 1918, probably in ships' ballast soil; the red imported fire ant arrived between 1933 and 1941. By 1958 they were in all or part of 141 counties in eight states. They are now in all 67 Alabama counties.Then and now: land, sky and wildlife, section 19; Encyclopedia of Alabama; USDA APHIS; Callcott and Collins, 1996Documented

People, law, and health 9

  1. 1875 to 1928
    Convict leasing, 1875 to 1928. The state and its counties leased prisoners for profit, overwhelmingly Black men, many arrested under laws like the vagrancy acts. By the 1880s nearly all of them worked in coal mines around Birmingham, and annual death rates among state prisoners ran 4 to 5%. Alabama ended it in 1928, the last state to do so. Whether the Brookside mine ever worked leased convicts: Unresolved.Then and now, Part D, section 40; Encyclopedia of Alabama, Convict-lease system; Equal Justice InitiativeDocumented
  2. 1877 to 1950
    Lynching in Jefferson County. The Equal Justice Initiative documents 29 racial terror lynchings of African Americans in Jefferson County from 1877 to 1950, the most of any Alabama county and tied for eighth among all counties in the twelve Southern states it studied. Names, dates and places: still to read from the county supplement.Then and now, Part D, section 46; EJI, Lynching in AmericaDocumented
  3. 1901 to 1940
    The 1901 constitution and the poll tax. The convention of 1901 was called to disfranchise Black voters without disfranchising whites. Section 194 required every voter aged 21 to 45 to pay a cumulative poll tax of $1.50 a year; with literacy, property and residency tests it shut out many poor white men as well. Approved 11 Nov 1901, 108,613 to 81,734. By 1940 it had disfranchised more white voters than Black in raw numbers (Glenn Feldman); Black citizens were shut out at far higher rates. Whether Nin or Bud paid the tax and voted: Unresolved.Then and now, Part D, section 45; Encyclopedia of Alabama, Alabama Constitution of 1901; Brennan Center, State Court ReportDocumented
  4. 1906 to 1940
    Pellagra. Dr. George H. Searcy reported epidemic pellagra at Mount Vernon, Alabama, in 1906. In 1915 Joseph Goldberger produced it in volunteers at a Mississippi prison farm with the meat, meal and molasses diet, and with Edgar Sydenstricker traced it to cotton monoculture: families who put all their land in cotton stopped growing vegetables and keeping stock. One estimate: more than 3 million affected and more than 100,000 dead in the South, 1907 to the early 1940s. Any pellagra in the family: Unresolved.Then and now, Part D, section 43; UAB Reynolds Historical Library; Science History Institute; NautilusDocumented
  5. 1909 to 1914
    The hookworm campaign. The Rockefeller Sanitary Commission's surveys found about 40% of Southern schoolchildren infected. In Alabama, with the State Board of Health, it paid for treatment of more than 13,000 people in 17 counties. Whether Jefferson County was one of them: Unresolved.Then and now, Part D, section 42; Encyclopedia of Alabama, Hookworm disease in Alabama; Bleakley, 2007Documented
  6. 1911
    The Banner mine explosion, 1911. An explosion at the Banner mine killed more than 120 leased county prisoners (123 or 128, sources differ), nearly all Black men. The date usually given is 8 April (to verify). In 1912 TCI announced it would use only free labor in its mines.Then and now, Part D, section 40; Encyclopedia of Alabama, Convict-lease systemDocumented
  7. 1914 to 1920
    Extension agents. The Smith-Lever Act of 1914 funded county farm agents through the land-grant colleges. Alabama's service was organized through Alabama Polytechnic Institute (now Auburn), John F. Duggar first director, 1914 to 1920. Sources differ on a 1914 or 1915 start. The service was segregated: federal money ran through white schools, and Black farmers were served separately and with less.Then and now, Part D, section 44; Encyclopedia of Alabama, Alabama Cooperative Extension System; Auburn University ArchivesDocumented
  8. 1915 to 1930
    The second Klan, 1915 to 1930. The second Ku Klux Klan was strong in Birmingham. Detail still to write up from the Encyclopedia of Alabama entry.Then and now, Part D, section 39; Encyclopedia of Alabama, Ku Klux Klan in Alabama, 1915 to 1930Documented
  9. 1919 to 1920
    The vote for women. The Women's Anti-Ratification League of Alabama formed on 17 Jun 1919. On 22 Sep 1919 Alabama became the second state to reject the Nineteenth Amendment. Women voted from 1920 under federal ratification. Whether Lela voted: Unresolved.Then and now, Part D, section 47; Encyclopedia of Alabama, Women's Anti-Ratification League; National Park ServiceDocumented

The Country line 9

  1. 1876 to 1914
    The emigration from Italy and Sicily. Italy lost 13.9 million emigrants from 1876 to 1914. Sicily sent about 1.1 million in 1901 to 1915, about 90% to the United States, and more than a quarter of the roughly 4 million Italians who reached the U.S. from 1880 to 1920 were Sicilian. About half of Italian emigrants to the U.S. in 1876 to 1914 later went home. Sicilian figures from secondary sources.Then and now, Part D, section 72; Wikipedia, Sicilian Americans; Cavaioli, Italian immigrationDocumented
  2. 1881 to 1912
    Favara and the sulphur mines. Favara is a mining town of the province of Girgenti (Agrigento since 1927), in the sulphur country of south-central Sicily. The sulphur was carried out of the pits on the backs of boys called carusi. An 1881 study found carusi, mostly aged 7 to 20, were the largest group of workers in the province's mines, with loads up to about 110 pounds. Families took cash advances against a boy's labor, a debt that often could not be paid off. Child labor in the mines was illegal from the 1860s and went on into the 1920s. Cheap American sulphur from Louisiana and Texas undercut the trade from the 1890s. Whether Charlie or his father worked the sulphur: Unresolved.Then and now, Part D, section 70; La Gazzetta Italiana; Balarm; Passerino EditoreDocumented
  3. 1891 to 1910
    Lynchings of Italians in the Gulf South. New Orleans, 14 Mar 1891: a mob broke into the parish prison and killed 11 Italian men. Leaders named in the record: William Parkerson, Walter Denegre, James D. Houston and John C. Wickliffe; John M. Parker and Walter C. Flower are named among those taking part (Parker's role to check). No one was prosecuted. Theodore Roosevelt wrote to his sister that he thought it "rather a good thing." Tallulah, Louisiana, 20 Jul 1899: Giuseppe, Pasquale and Francesco Di Fatta, Giovanni Cerami and Rosario Fiduccia, Sicilians, hanged by a mob. Erwin, Mississippi, 1901: Giovanni and Vincenzo Serio reported hanged. Tampa, 20 Sep 1910: Angelo Albano and Castenge Ficarrotta, Sicilians, lynched during a cigar strike. No Alabama case found yet: Unresolved.Then and now, Part D, section 57; Smithsonian, 2019; Foreign Relations of the United States, 1900 and 1901; Luconi, Florida Historical Quarterly 88Documented
  4. 1907
    Peonage at Sunnyside, Arkansas. Italian families came to Sunnyside plantation, Chicot County, owing their passage. In 1907 the Justice Department's investigator, Mary Grace Quackenbos, found peonage. LeRoy Percy, who had leased and managed Sunnyside, disputed her, and his influence is credited with shelving the report.Then and now, Part D, section 58; Encyclopedia of Arkansas, Peonage; History News NetworkDocumented
  5. 1907 to 1917
    John L. Burnett and the literacy test. John L. Burnett of Alabama sat on the Dillingham Commission from 1907 and chaired the House immigration committee by 1911. He worked to restrict immigration from southern and eastern Europe; his literacy test became law over Wilson's veto on 5 Feb 1917. He estimated it would keep out about 40% of Mediterranean immigrants. Charlie could read and had landed in 1912, so it did not touch him.Then and now, Part D, section 60; Encyclopedia of Alabama, John Lawson Burnett; Wikipedia, Immigration Act of 1917Documented
  6. 1910
    Italians in Birmingham, 1910. Almost 2,000 by 1910, with a Little Italy in Ensley by the TCI works and settlements at Thomas, Blocton and East Lake. At Cardiff, Price and Williamson hired from Wales and Scotland, then France and Italy; the town marker names the Tombrellos from Sicily.Then and now, Part D, section 61; The Homewood Star; WBHM, 2005; fivemile heritage fileDocumented
  7. 1911
    The Dictionary of Races or Peoples, 1911. Written by Daniel and Elnora Folkmar for the Dillingham Commission, it split Italians into northern and southern "races" and called southerners excitable and impulsive. Italians were the only group split in two. The findings fed the 1920s quotas.Then and now, Part D, section 60; American Heritage, Genes, brains, and bunkDocumented
  8. 1918 to 1922
    Julia and the Expatriation Act. Under the Expatriation Act of 2 Mar 1907, "any American woman who marries a foreigner shall take the nationality of her husband." Julia, born at Cardiff in 1903, married Charlie, an Italian national, in October 1918, so by that law she stopped being a U.S. citizen at 15. The Cable Act of 22 Sep 1922 ended the rule for later marriages; women already caught by it had to naturalize to get their citizenship back. When and how Julia got hers back: Unresolved.Then and now, Part D, section 68; National Archives, Prologue, Spring 2014Computed
  9. 1919
    Alabama's convent inspection law, 1919. On a petition of 25 citizens, a state commission could send sheriffs to search Catholic convents at any hour. Georgia, Florida and Arkansas passed similar laws. Statute text, sponsor and repeal: Unresolved.Then and now, Part D, section 62; History Ireland, Bigotry in 'Bama (secondary)Documented

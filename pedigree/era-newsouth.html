What lived and grew here, and the sky, in these years16
  1. to 1883
    Sun time. Before standard time each town kept its own sun time. The railroads' time zones took effect 18 Nov 1883 (to verify). William died 28 Oct 1883, three weeks before.Then and now: land, sky and wildlife, section 2General history
  2. to 1889
    Beaver. Before the trappers, beavers dammed Alabama's creeks into shallow ponds with meadows at their edges, grazed by deer and, in their time, elk and bison.Then and now: land, sky and wildlife, section 12; Bham Now, 2019Documented
  3. to 1900
    Passenger pigeon. Wintered across the South in huge flocks and roosts. Extinct in the wild by the early 1900s; the last known bird died at the Cincinnati Zoo, 1 Sep 1914. An Alabama or Jefferson County account is still to find.Then and now: land, sky and wildlife, section 7 (to be sourced)General history
  4. to 1903
    American chestnut. Ranged from Maine to Alabama and bore heavy nut crops every year, unlike oaks, which boom and bust. Chestnut and chestnut oak are among the trees the 1821 surveyors named most often on the home sections.Then and now: land, sky and wildlife, sections 9 and 13; Penn State Extension; WorldAtlasDocumented
  5. to 1909
    Black bear. Historically native throughout Alabama; nearly extirpated in the early 1900s.Then and now: land, sky and wildlife, section 15; National Park Service, Little River CanyonDocumented
  6. to 1918
    Carolina parakeet. America's only native parrot. Audubon warned in 1831 that where they had been plentiful scarcely any were to be seen. The last captive bird, Incas, died at the Cincinnati Zoo on 21 Feb 1918. Whether parakeets reached the Jefferson County uplands: Unresolved.Then and now: land, sky and wildlife, section 17; Smithsonian; Kevin Burgio, University of ConnecticutDocumented
  7. to 1922
    Red wolf. Once lived in a variety of habitats statewide, in small groups; also took sheep, goats, pigs, and sometimes calves. Reported near extinction in Alabama in 1921 and gone by the early 1920s.Outdoor Alabama, Red wolfDocumented
  8. to 1924
    White-tailed deer: the decline. Alabama had a thriving deer population before and during the colonial period; unregulated hunting, market hunting and habitat loss then pushed them down. In 1908 deer were present in 38 of the state's 67 counties, "only a few thousand"; by 1922, in only 11. Estimates of the low differ (about 2,000 or about 10,000): Unresolved.Then and now: land, sky and wildlife, section 10; AP, 2017, citing Davis, The White-Tailed Deer in Alabama (1979); Outdoor Alabama; Discovering AlabamaDocumented
  9. to 1939
    Bald eagle. Present in Alabama in William's time (General history). Killed by people, and later harmed by pesticides, in the early 1900s; killing them was not outlawed until the Bald Eagle Protection Act of 1940.Alabama Ornithological Society; Alabama TodayGeneral history
  10. to 1940
    Mosquitoes and malaria. Malaria was endemic across large parts of the South until the 1930s. A study of Alabama's 67 counties found that drainage projects (much of it WPA work) and new county health departments brought it down. The federal agency that became the CDC was created during World War II to fight Southern malaria. Malaria in Jefferson County and in the family: Unresolved.Then and now: land, sky and wildlife, section 22; Sledge and Mohler, American Journal of Public Health, 2013; NPR, 2013Documented
  11. to 1942
    Wild turkey. Habitat loss, unregulated hunting and market hunting brought the wild turkey to the edge of extinction in Alabama; about 10,000 were thought to remain in the early 1900s. A somewhat isolated population held on around the Bankhead National Forest.Then and now: land, sky and wildlife, section 11; Alabama Living (retired ADCNR biologist Steve Barnett); Outdoor AlabamaDocumented
  12. to 2026
    Chuck-will's-widow. A native summer resident that nests on the ground in open woods (General history, to be sourced).Then and now: land, sky and wildlife, section 6General history
  13. 1829 to 2011
    Periodical cicadas: Brood XIX years. Counting back by 13 years from 2024, Brood XIX's emergence years are 2011, 1998, 1985, 1972, 1959, 1946, 1933, 1920, 1907, 1894, 1881, 1868, 1855, 1842 and 1829. Whether the brood emerges at Mount Olive: Unresolved.Then and now: land, sky and wildlife, section 21Computed
  14. 1882
    The Great Comet of 1882. The Great Comet of September 1882.Then and now: land, sky and wildlife, section 28; BhamwikiDocumented
  15. 1890 to 1949
    Beaver: trapped out. Extremely scarce in Alabama by 1890. Only 41 beaver dams were found in the state in 1929, and the population in the 1930s was about 500 animals. Restocking began in the 1940s.Then and now: land, sky and wildlife, section 12; Hill, Vertebrate Pest Conference; Alabama Cooperative ExtensionDocumented
  16. 1890 to 2026
    European starling and house sparrow. The starling was released in Central Park, New York, in 1890 by the American Acclimatization Society, which also introduced the house sparrow. Their spread helped bring about the Lacey Act of 1900. "The Starling in Montgomery, Alabama" was published in The Auk in 1918. When either reached Jefferson County: Unresolved.Then and now: land, sky and wildlife, section 18; NPS, Chattahoochee; Carolina Bird Club; SORA (Auk 1918)Documented

People, law, and health 3

  1. 1875 to 1928
    Convict leasing, 1875 to 1928. The state and its counties leased prisoners for profit, overwhelmingly Black men, many arrested under laws like the vagrancy acts. By the 1880s nearly all of them worked in coal mines around Birmingham, and annual death rates among state prisoners ran 4 to 5%. Alabama ended it in 1928, the last state to do so. Whether the Brookside mine ever worked leased convicts: Unresolved.Then and now, Part D, section 40; Encyclopedia of Alabama, Convict-lease system; Equal Justice InitiativeDocumented
  2. 1877 to 1950
    Lynching in Jefferson County. The Equal Justice Initiative documents 29 racial terror lynchings of African Americans in Jefferson County from 1877 to 1950, the most of any Alabama county and tied for eighth among all counties in the twelve Southern states it studied. Names, dates and places: still to read from the county supplement.Then and now, Part D, section 46; EJI, Lynching in AmericaDocumented
  3. 1896
    Rural Free Delivery, 7 Dec 1896. RFD began nationally on 1 Oct 1896 in West Virginia; Alabama's first route started at Opelika on 7 Dec 1896. When it reached Mount Olive or Gardendale: Unresolved.Then and now, Part D, section 41; USPS Historian, First rural routesDocumented

The Country line 4

  1. 1876 to 1914
    The emigration from Italy and Sicily. Italy lost 13.9 million emigrants from 1876 to 1914. Sicily sent about 1.1 million in 1901 to 1915, about 90% to the United States, and more than a quarter of the roughly 4 million Italians who reached the U.S. from 1880 to 1920 were Sicilian. About half of Italian emigrants to the U.S. in 1876 to 1914 later went home. Sicilian figures from secondary sources.Then and now, Part D, section 72; Wikipedia, Sicilian Americans; Cavaioli, Italian immigrationDocumented
  2. 1881 to 1912
    Favara and the sulphur mines. Favara is a mining town of the province of Girgenti (Agrigento since 1927), in the sulphur country of south-central Sicily. The sulphur was carried out of the pits on the backs of boys called carusi. An 1881 study found carusi, mostly aged 7 to 20, were the largest group of workers in the province's mines, with loads up to about 110 pounds. Families took cash advances against a boy's labor, a debt that often could not be paid off. Child labor in the mines was illegal from the 1860s and went on into the 1920s. Cheap American sulphur from Louisiana and Texas undercut the trade from the 1890s. Whether Charlie or his father worked the sulphur: Unresolved.Then and now, Part D, section 70; La Gazzetta Italiana; Balarm; Passerino EditoreDocumented
  3. 1891 to 1910
    Lynchings of Italians in the Gulf South. New Orleans, 14 Mar 1891: a mob broke into the parish prison and killed 11 Italian men. Leaders named in the record: William Parkerson, Walter Denegre, James D. Houston and John C. Wickliffe; John M. Parker and Walter C. Flower are named among those taking part (Parker's role to check). No one was prosecuted. Theodore Roosevelt wrote to his sister that he thought it "rather a good thing." Tallulah, Louisiana, 20 Jul 1899: Giuseppe, Pasquale and Francesco Di Fatta, Giovanni Cerami and Rosario Fiduccia, Sicilians, hanged by a mob. Erwin, Mississippi, 1901: Giovanni and Vincenzo Serio reported hanged. Tampa, 20 Sep 1910: Angelo Albano and Castenge Ficarrotta, Sicilians, lynched during a cigar strike. No Alabama case found yet: Unresolved.Then and now, Part D, section 57; Smithsonian, 2019; Foreign Relations of the United States, 1900 and 1901; Luconi, Florida Historical Quarterly 88Documented
  4. 1893 to 1896
    The Fasci Siciliani. Leagues of peasants, sulphur workers and laborers across Sicily. After violent clashes in December 1893, Prime Minister Francesco Crispi declared a state of siege on 3 Jan 1894, sent about 40,000 troops and set up military courts; about a thousand people were jailed without trial and leaders were convicted. An amnesty came in March 1896, the year Charlie was born. Favara's part: Unresolved.Then and now, Part D, section 71; Britannica, Fascio siciliano; Wikipedia, Fasci SicilianiDocumented

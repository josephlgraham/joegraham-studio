Native ground

before 1814 · family chapters: Thompson and Hannah (1771 to 1819)

Before the Creek cession. The area that became Jefferson County was part of a large neutral ground between the Creek, Cherokee, Chickasaw, and Choctaw nations.

Bhamwiki, Jefferson County; Encyclopedia of Alabama, Jefferson County

Territorial period

1814 to 1819 · family chapters: Thompson and Hannah (1771 to 1819)

The Treaty of Fort Jackson (9 Aug 1814) took the land from the Creeks. It became part of Monroe County, Mississippi Territory, then Alabama Territory (1817). Jefferson County was created 13 Dec 1819; Alabama became a state the next day.

Encyclopedia of Alabama, Jefferson County and Territorial Period and Early Statehood

1820 to 1835 · family chapters: William and Rebecca (1820 to 1845)

People, law, and health

  • The Land Act of 1820 and the forty (public land for cash). Public land at $1.25 an acre, cash only, minimum 80 acres. In 1832 the minimum was cut to 40 acres, making the cheapest farm $50; it stayed there until the Homestead Act of 1862. People without cash, and the enslaved, who could own nothing, got no land from it. Documented

People, law, and health

  • Lynching in Jefferson County (29 documented). The Equal Justice Initiative documents 29 racial terror lynchings of African Americans in Jefferson County from 1877 to 1950, the most of any Alabama county and tied for eighth among all counties in the twelve Southern states it studied.     Then and now, Part D, section 46; EJI, Lynching in America
  • Birmingham's racial zoning (by permit). Birmingham's 1926 zoning ordinance zoned by race, sidestepping the Supreme Court's 1917 ruling against racial zoning; it stood until 1951, the South's longest-lasting such law. Planners could revoke permits to stop Black housing in white areas. Documented
    Then and now, Part D, section 51; Bhamwiki, Segregation ordinances; HMdb; Connerly, 2005
  • The Tuskegee syphilis study (untreated). From 1932 to 1972 the U.S. Public Health Service, with Tuskegee Institute and the Macon County and Alabama health departments, followed about 399 Black men with late-stage syphilis and 201 without, without consent and without treatment, though the men believed they were being treated. It ended in 1972 after Peter Buxtun took it to the press. President Clinton apologized in 1997. Documented
    Then and now, Part D, section 52; Encyclopedia of Alabama, Tuskegee syphilis study
  • Social Security and the farm (farm work left out). The 1935 act covered mainly workers in nonfarm industry and commerce; all farm work was left out. The 1950 amendments brought in about 850,000 farm workers and 650,000 domestic workers; the 1954 amendments let farm operators report self-employment income. Whether the exclusions were made for racial reasons is argued (sources disagree); the effect fell hardest on Black Southern workers either way. Documented
    Then and now, Part D, section 49; SSA Bulletin, 1950 and 1954 coverage articles

  • The dynamite from Leon Negron's store, 4 Sep 1963 (the court record). In November 1976 Robert Chambliss told Birmingham police that Don Luna asked him to get dynamite at Leon Negron's store, and that he got it there on 4 Sep 1963, eleven days before the Sixteenth Street bombing: a case, then an opened case, with a box of caps and a roll of fuse. This is Chambliss's account, as police repeated it in court; Negron never testified. The opinion also records that a "Lewis Negron" (spelled "Louis Negron" a page later) was dead by the 1977 trial.

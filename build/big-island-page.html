The Big Island

Hawaiʻi Island · January 2018, January 2022, and February 2024

Hawaiʻi Island is the youngest island in the chain and by far the biggest: about 4,028 square miles, nearly twice the size of all the other Hawaiian Islands put together. Five volcanoes built it, and two of them, Kīlauea and Mauna Loa, are still adding land.

It is also where modern Hawaiʻi begins. Kamehameha, the chief who united the islands, was born in North Kohala. Captain Cook died at Kealakekua Bay. And a little over a century after Kamehameha built a kingdom, that kingdom was gone. The photos here are the postcard. The small print under each one is the rest of the story.

Some backgroundBefore the stops

How a kingdom became a vacation

The short version, from a king born in Kohala to a state where more Native Hawaiians now live away than at home.

A short history of the island
Around 1758Kamehameha is born in North Kohala, near ʻUpolu Point.
February 14, 1779Captain James Cook is killed at Kealakekua Bay, a year after his first landing at Waimea on Kauaʻi. (The Waimea up in this island’s ranch country is a different town.)
1810Kamehameha finishes uniting the islands into one kingdom.
1848 and 1850The Māhele turns land into private property, and a new law lets foreigners buy it. By the end of the century, foreigners and large estates own most of the land that isn’t government land.
1893American and European businessmen, backed by U.S. Marines, overthrow Queen Liliʻuokalani.
1896The new republic makes English the language of instruction in every school.
1897 and 1898More than 21,000 Hawaiians sign petitions against annexation, and the treaty fails in the Senate. Congress annexes Hawaiʻi anyway, by a simple joint resolution.
1959Statehood. Tourism soon replaces sugar as the islands’ business.
1964The Army leases about 23,000 acres of state land on this island for 65 years, for $1.
1983 and 1987Hawaiian-language teachers start the Pūnana Leo preschools. Four years later, Hawaiian is allowed back as a language of instruction in public schools.
1993Congress formally apologizes for the overthrow.
2019Thousands gather to block the road up Mauna Kea and stop another telescope on the summit.
2020For the first time, the census counts more Native Hawaiians living outside Hawaiʻi than in it.

1North of Kona2018, late afternoon

A cave on the side of the road

A lava tube right beside the highway north of the Kona airport. Everybody went scrambling. It has been closed off since.

Hawaiians used tubes like this for shelter, water, and burial. Some things are closed off for good reasons.

Lava tubes and Hualālai
How tubes formThe top of a lava flow cools and crusts over while hot lava keeps moving underneath. When the flow drains out, it leaves a tunnel behind.
The mountainHualālai, 8,271 feet, the volcano behind Kona. It last erupted in 1800 and 1801.
Built on lavaKona’s airport sits on top of one of those 1801 flows, which ran all the way to the sea and formed Keāhole Point.
Stay outTubes collapse, and some hold burial sites. If one is closed off, it is closed for a reason.

2Waikoloa2022, afternoon

King of the rock

Lots of goats around Waikoloa, and a few donkeys too. This one found the best seat in the house.

The first goats came as gifts from European ships in the 1700s. Gifts from ships have a way of staying.

Goats and donkeys
GoatsNot native. They arrived on European ships in the late 1700s and went wild across the islands, where they eat native plants down to the ground.
DonkeysThe Waikoloa donkeys descend from the “Kona Nightingales,” donkeys brought in during the early 1900s to haul coffee on Kona’s steep farms. The nickname came from their braying at night.
Out of workAfter World War II, jeeps and tractors took over the hauling, and many donkeys were turned loose.

2ʻAnaehoʻomalu Bay2018, afternoon

A pool that is half ocean

It looks like a puddle. It is actually one of the rarest habitats in Hawaiʻi.

Chiefs once raised fish in the royal ponds just behind this beach. Today the ponds sit inside a resort.

Anchialine pools
What they arePools in the lava near the shore, fed from underneath by fresh groundwater and by seawater seeping in through the rock. The water is brackish and rises and falls with the tide.
Who lives hereTiny red shrimp called ʻōpaeʻula, along with small fish and snails. Hawaiians used the shrimp as bait and fish food.
ProtectedThe Waikoloa Anchialine Pond Preservation Area was set aside in 1985.
Next doorKuʻualiʻi and Kahapapa, two old Hawaiian fishponds right behind the beach.
Look onlyDon’t swim, wade, or put hands in. Sunscreen and soap harm the shrimp.

Status updateFebruary 2024

Whales seen: lots 🐋
Favorite moment ever: this one

No photos, just the memory. Humpbacks from the beach at the hotel, again in Hilo, and again at sunset up at Lapakahi. Hard to top.

From 1840 to 1860, more than 400 whaling ships a year put in at Hawaiian ports. The whales came back. That part of the story has a happy ending.

Humpback season in Hawaiʻi
WhenThe first whales show up in late November. The peak is January through March, and most are gone by April.
Why hereThey spend summer feeding in Alaska and come to warm, shallow Hawaiian water to mate, give birth, and raise their calves.
The countThe Sanctuary Ocean Count happens every year on the last Saturday of January, February, and March. Anyone can volunteer to count from shore. On February 28, 2026, volunteers counted 1,935 whales statewide, 244 of them off Hawaiʻi Island.
Give them roomFederal rules require staying at least 100 yards from humpbacks in Hawaiʻi, on the water and in the air.

4Mauna Kea Beach Hotel2024, breakfast

Coffee with a view

Breakfast at the Mauna Kea Beach Hotel. Coffee, the ocean, and nowhere to be.

The hotel is named for the mountain behind it. In 2019, thousands of people, many of them elders, blocked the road up that mountain for weeks to stop one more telescope from going on its sacred summit. They called themselves kiaʻi: protectors.

The Mauna Kea Beach Hotel
OpenedJuly 1965, the first resort on the island.
The origin storyLaurance Rockefeller decided to build here while swimming in Kaunaʻoa Bay.
The price tagAbout $15 million for 154 rooms, which made it the most expensive hotel ever built at the time. Esquire called it one of the three greatest hotels in the world.

5Lapakahi, North Kohala2022, sunset

Sunset over an old fishing village

The sun going down over the Kohala Coast, where I watched whales at sunset.

For 600 years this land was shared, each village with its own slice from the mountains to the sea. In 1848 it became property that could be bought and sold. By 1900, foreigners and big estates owned most of the private land.

Lapakahi State Historical Park
What it isA partly restored Hawaiian fishing village more than 600 years old, with stone walls, house sites, canoe sheds, and fishing shrines along a one-mile loop trail.
How it workedThe village was part of an ahupuaʻa, a slice of land running from the mountains down to the sea, so one community had farmland, forest, and fishing grounds.
Why it emptiedThe freshwater table dropped in the 1800s, and the village was abandoned.
OffshoreA Marine Life Conservation District, and a good place to look for whales in winter.
Up the roadKamehameha’s birthplace, near ʻUpolu Point at the island’s northern tip.

6Waipiʻo Valley Lookout2018, midday

The Valley of the Kings

Cliffs, a black-sand beach, and a valley floor that used to hold thousands of people.

Kamehameha grew up down there. A century after he united the islands, there was no Hawaiian king anywhere.

Waipiʻo Valley quick facts
HistoryOnce the political and religious center of the island and the boyhood home of Kamehameha. Thousands of people lived here. Today fewer than 100 do.
1946The April 1 tsunami sent waves about 55 feet high into the valley. Many farmers never came back.
Visiting nowThe lookout is open. The steep road down has been closed to visitors since 2022 because of rockfall; only residents, permitted tours, and Native Hawaiian practitioners can use it. I went in 2018, before the closure, and only looked from the top.

7ʻAkaka Falls2024, late morning

442 feet, one drop

A misty morning, and the falls dropping straight into the rain forest.

For a hundred years, sugar cane covered this coast. The last cane truck rolled through Honokaʻa in 1994, and the Hāmākua plantation land was sold.

ʻAkaka Falls quick facts
Height442 feet in a single, uninterrupted plunge.
The waterKolekole Stream, collecting rain off the slopes above the town of Honomū.
The walkA paved 0.4-mile loop through bamboo, wild ginger, and giant rainforest plants. Allow about half an hour.

8Hilo2022, late morning

Rainbow Falls

Right at the edge of town. No hike required.

Waiānuenue means “rainbow water.” From 1896 to 1987, Hawaiian was not a language of instruction in the public schools, and by 1985 only 32 children under 18 spoke it. Then a few teachers refused to let it go. In 1983 they started the Pūnana Leo, preschools run entirely in Hawaiian, and today kids learn in Hawaiian again.

Waiānuenue quick facts
The nameWaiānuenue means “rainbow water.” On sunny mornings around 10 a.m., rainbows form in the mist.
SizeAbout 80 feet tall, on the Wailuku River.
The legendThe lava cave behind the falls is said to be the home of Hina, mother of the demigod Maui.

8Hilo Farmers Market2018, afternoon

What’s on the table

One table, and a lesson in what grows here.

All this fruit, and Hawaiʻi still imports about 85 to 90 percent of its food.

On the table
RambutanThe red, hairy ones. The name comes from the Malay word for hair. Peel it and the inside is sweet and white, a lot like a lychee.
ʻUlu (breadfruit)The big green globes. A canoe plant, carried to Hawaiʻi by Polynesian voyagers and eaten steamed, baked, or boiled, a lot like a potato.
Kalo (taro)The brown roots. Pounded into poi, and Hawaiʻi’s official state plant since 2025.
Also hereTangerines, longan, and zucchini.
The marketStarted in 1988 by Richard “Mike” Rankin and four other farmers selling out of their trucks. It now has more than 200 vendors. Wednesdays and Saturdays are the big days.

9Kīlauea summitJanuary 10, 2018, evening
1 of 3

Waiting for dark at the volcano

Waiting at the Jaggar Museum overlook for the crater to start glowing.

The park was created in 1916, when Hawaiʻi was a territory with no vote in the Congress that created it.

Kīlauea quick facts
What it isOne of the most active volcanoes on Earth, inside Hawaiʻi Volcanoes National Park.
HalemaʻumaʻuThe crater inside the summit caldera. In Hawaiian tradition it is the home of Pele, goddess of volcanoes.
The museumThe Jaggar Museum’s last day open was May 11, 2018. The 2018 collapse damaged it beyond repair, and it was taken down in 2024.
Visiting nowThe restored Uēkahuna overlook, where the museum used to stand, reopened in 2025.

9HalemaʻumaʻuThen and now
🔍Then and now. My photo from January 2018, then what happened to the same crater afterward. Tap the dates.

The lava lake, four months before it vanished

Just before dark on January 10, 2018. That orange glow is the lava lake inside Halemaʻumaʻu, active since 2008 and one of the biggest sights in the park. Nobody standing here knew it had four months left.

In Hawaiian tradition this crater is the home of Pele, and the land is hers before it is anyone’s. In 2018 she added 875 acres and took back more than 700 homes.

The 2018 eruption in numbers
WhenMay through August 2018, on the lower East Rift Zone in Puna and at the summit.
The lakeBegan draining in early May and was gone from view by May 10.
The craterDropped from about 280 feet deep to about 1,600 feet, and more than doubled in width.
HomesMore than 700 destroyed, and more than 2,000 people displaced.
New land875 acres added to the island.
EarthquakesMore than 60,000 between May and August.

Status updateFebruary 9, 2024, 10:06 a.m.

Earthquakes felt: 1 📳
How long it lasted: lonnnng

I was working, recording the last speaker at a conference, when it hit. My first earthquake, and it went on much longer than I expected.

The islands are slowly sinking under their own weight. Geologically speaking, that is what I felt.

What it was
SizeThe first alert said magnitude 6.3. The USGS Hawaiian Volcano Observatory later put it at 5.7.
WhereAbout a mile southwest of Pāhala, in Kaʻū on the south side of the island, 23 miles underground.
FeltAcross Hawaiʻi Island and Maui and on parts of Oʻahu. Instruments recorded very strong shaking near the center, but no damage was expected.
Why it happenedNot the volcanoes. The sheer weight of the islands bends the ocean plate underneath them, and deep quakes like this one release that strain.
TsunamiNone. Hawaiʻi’s emergency agency said so right away.

Boston

The Seaport, the Freedom Trail, Back Bay, and the Fenway · 2018, 2023, and 2025

Boston started as the Shawmut Peninsula, in the homeland of the Massachusett people, where English Puritans settled in 1630. A century and a half later, it was the town where colonists argued, then fought, over being taxed by a Parliament they had no vote in.

Then Boston kept building. It filled in its own harbor, opened a library free to all, and made fortunes in the China trade. The Freedom Trail tells the story of liberty. The photos here are the postcard. The small print under each one asks who got the liberty, who paid for it, and who made money along the way.

Liberty, and who paid for it

The short version: who had a vote, who paid, and who pushed back.

A short history of Boston
Before 1630The Shawmut Peninsula, a small, nearly island piece of land in the homeland of the Massachusett.
1630English Puritans settle the peninsula and name it Boston.
1675 and 1676During King Philip’s War, the colony holds somewhere between 500 and 1,100 Christian Native people on Deer Island in the harbor through the winter. Many do not survive.
1761A girl of about seven arrives in Boston on the slave ship Phillis. The Wheatley family buys her and names her after the ship.
1765The Stamp Act. A crowd hangs the stamp collector in effigy from a big elm. It becomes the Liberty Tree.
1768Customs officials seize John Hancock’s sloop Liberty on smuggling charges, and a riot follows.
1773The Tea Act makes legal tea cheaper, not dearer. On December 16, about 340 chests go into the harbor.
1774Parliament closes the port of Boston and ends much of Massachusetts’ self-government.
1790s to 1850Boston merchants grow rich in the China trade, paying for tea with opium that China had banned.
1848The Boston Public Library is founded, the first large free municipal library in the country.
1857 to 1900The Back Bay and the Fens are filled in with gravel brought by train.
1898More than 21,000 Hawaiians have signed petitions against annexation. Congress annexes Hawaiʻi anyway.
1945 and 1959The Red Sox try out Jackie Robinson and sign no one. Fourteen years later they become the last major league team to field a Black player.
1990Thieves take 13 works from the Isabella Stewart Gardner Museum. None have been recovered.
2017 and 2018The Boston Globe reports that the Seaport, built with more than $18 billion in public money, is 89 percent white. The next year, Yawkey Way goes back to being Jersey Street.

Over Boston Harbor2018, early afternoon

Coming in over the harbor

A window seat, a low cloud ceiling, and the whole south side of the harbor laid out like a map, with the Harbor Islands lined up on the horizon.

In the fall of 1675, the colony rounded up Native people who had converted to Christianity and shipped them out to Deer Island, in this harbor, with little food or shelter for the winter. Many did not live to see spring.

2Status updateDecember 16, 1773

Why the tea went in the water 🫖

Here is the part the gift shop skips: the Tea Act did not make tea more expensive. It made legal tea cheaper.

By 1773, most of the tea Americans drank was smuggled Dutch tea, about 86 percent of it. The Tea Act let the East India Company ship straight to the colonies and cut out the middlemen, so taxed, legal tea would sell for about 2 shillings a pound, a penny under the smuggled kind. The black market was about to lose its price advantage.

The people with the most to lose were the smugglers and the merchants who hadn’t been picked to sell the Company’s tea. Wealthy smugglers such as John Hancock were among them. One of the three tea ships, the Eleanor, belonged to a Boston merchant who was himself a smuggler.

The principle was real too. Parliament kept a tax of three pence a pound, and the colonists had no vote in Parliament. Historians still argue about the mix of principle and profit. Both were on the wharf that night.

Boston’s merchants didn’t give up on tea. Within a generation, firms like Perkins & Company were paying for it by smuggling opium into China, which had banned it. Those fortunes helped build the Perkins School for the Blind, Massachusetts General Hospital, and the Boston Athenaeum.

The Tea Party by the numbers
The meeting5,000 to 7,000 people crowded into Old South Meeting House, about a third of the town. The usual property rules for town meetings were dropped, so laborers, sailors, and apprentices were in the room.
The shipsDartmouth, Eleanor, and Beaver.
The tea340 chests (342 in the old newspaper count), 92,616 pounds, valued by the East India Company at £9,659.
How longAbout three hours. A padlock broken along the way was replaced.
The disguisesA few men dressed as Mohawks. Most didn’t bother.
The billParliament closed the port of Boston in 1774.
🌺Taxation without a vote, again. In 1898, the same country annexed Hawaiʻi over the petitions of more than 21,000 Hawaiians. See the history on the Big Island trip.

3Central Burying Ground, Boston Common2018, afternoon

Slate and bare trees

Early spring on the Common, before the leaves, when you can see every headstone.

Samuel Sprague, one of the men who dumped the tea, is buried here. So are British soldiers who died during the occupation of Boston that followed.

Central Burying Ground quick facts
Opened1756, on the Boylston Street side of Boston Common.
Also hereGilbert Stuart, who painted the portrait of George Washington on the one-dollar bill, and William Billings, who wrote the Revolutionary hymn “Chester.”

4The Liberty Tree siteWashington and Essex Streets

The tree that isn’t there 🌳

No photo for this one. There hasn’t been a tree to photograph since 1775.

It was a big elm on Orange Street, now Washington Street, planted around 1646. On August 14, 1765, Bostonians woke up to find an effigy of Andrew Oliver, the man picked to collect the new stamp tax, hanging from it. By December, Oliver had resigned, reportedly under the same tree. When the Stamp Act was repealed, people hung a copper sign on it that said Liberty Tree, and 150 lanterns.

It stayed the place to protest. In November 1773, crowds gathered here to demand that the tea consignees resign in public. Two of them were the governor’s sons. In 1775, during the occupation, British soldiers cut the tree down for firewood. It made 14 cords.

Town meeting had property rules for who could vote. The tree didn’t. Anyone could stand under it.

The Liberty Tree today
The markerA carved plaque from 1850, on the building at the corner of Washington and Essex Streets.
The plazaLiberty Tree Plaza opened on the spot in 2018. Pipes and wires underground mean a new tree can’t be planted there.

5Marshall Street and the Union Oyster House2023, evening

Cobblestones and a red sign

Old brick, cobblestones, and a neon oyster house sign, with the glass towers of downtown stacked up behind.

During the Revolution, Ebenezer Hancock paid the Continental Army out of this building. His brother John is the one with the famous signature, and the one whose sloop was seized for smuggling in 1768.

Union Oyster House quick facts
The buildingFinished around 1716.
The restaurantServing since October 7, 1826, the oldest continuously operating restaurant known in the country. It turns 200 on October 7, 2026.
UpstairsIsaiah Thomas printed his newspaper, the Massachusetts Spy, here in 1771. In 1796 the future King Louis Philippe of France lived upstairs in exile and taught French.
RegularsDaniel Webster, known for putting away at least six plates of oysters.

6Copley Square2023, night

Blue hour, blue bikes

Old South Church’s bell tower lit up over Boylston Street, and a row of Bluebikes waiting for morning.

Phillis Wheatley belonged to this congregation. In September 1773, her book of poems came out in London while she was still enslaved in Boston. She was freed that fall, a few weeks before the tea went in the harbor.

Old South Church quick facts
The congregationFounded in 1669. It met at Old South Meeting House, where the Tea Party meeting was held, and moved here in 1875.
The groundFreshly filled land. In 1773 this spot was part of the Back Bay.
The tower246 feet tall.
Its treasureOne of eleven surviving first editions of the Bay Psalm Book, printed in 1640, the first book printed in what became the United States.

6Boston Public Library2023, midday

Green lamps, all the way down

Bates Hall: a barrel-vaulted reading room with a whole runway of green lamps. You can walk in and sit down.

Carved over the front door: FREE TO ALL. The money that started the library came from Joshua Bates, a Massachusetts-born banker in London, who asked for exactly that.

Boston Public Library quick facts
Founded1848, the first large free municipal library in the United States. It opened in 1854 in a former schoolhouse.
This buildingDesigned by Charles Follen McKim and opened in 1895. McKim called it a “palace for the people.”
The nameBates Hall honors Joshua Bates, who gave $50,000 in 1852 and $50,000 more for books.

6Boston Public LibraryA few minutes later

Two lions on the stairs

Golden marble, a chandelier, murals, and a pair of lions keeping watch on the landing.

The lions are a war memorial. They honor the 2nd and 20th Massachusetts, two Union regiments from the Civil War.

The grand staircase
The lionsBy Louis Saint-Gaudens, left unpolished. Veterans of the two regiments liked them that way.
The muralsPierre Puvis de Chavannes, The Muses of Inspiration Hail the Spirit, the Harbinger of Light. His only murals on display outside France.

7Fenway Park2023, afternoon

Welcome to Fenway Park

Green steel, a welcome banner, and the Pru standing over the ballpark on a clear summer afternoon.

Until 2018, this was Yawkey Way, named for the owner whose Red Sox tried out Jackie Robinson here in 1945 and signed no one. In 1959 they became the last major league team to put a Black player on the field.

Fenway Park quick facts
OpenedApril 20, 1912. It is the oldest ballpark in the majors still in use.
The groundThe Fenway neighborhood was made in the late 1800s by filling in marsh, the Fens.
The Green MonsterThe left field wall, about 37 feet tall, painted green in 1947.
The firstPumpsie Green, the first Black player for the Red Sox, debuted on July 21, 1959.

8Museum of Fine Arts2018, evening

Me and Sargent

A mirror selfie under John Singer Sargent’s painted ceiling in the MFA rotunda.

Sargent painted Isabella Stewart Gardner in 1888. The portrait set Boston gossiping, and her husband asked her not to show it in public again while he was alive. It hung in a gallery she kept private.

The rotunda quick facts
The muralsPainted by Sargent from 1916 to 1925. The rotunda was unveiled in 1921, and the last pieces went up after his death.
The buildingThe MFA moved here, to Huntington Avenue, in 1909.

9Isabella Stewart Gardner Museum2023, afternoon

The nasturtiums are in

A Venetian palace courtyard under a glass roof, in full spring bloom. Look at the balconies on both sides: those long orange ribbons are the hanging nasturtiums.

The hanging nasturtiums
The traditionIsabella Stewart Gardner started it, to celebrate her birthday on April 14.
How long it takesAbout nine months. The vines start from seed in June and are trained in the museum’s greenhouses all winter.
How long they getUp to about 20 feet. The longest has hung around 25.
When to goThey hang for about three weeks, from late March into April.
The museumOpened in 1903, with the collection arranged by Gardner herself.

9Isabella Stewart Gardner MuseumA few minutes later, upstairs

A jungle under glass

From the upper galleries, the courtyard disappears behind leaves and sunlight.

At 1:24 in the morning on March 18, 1990, two men dressed as police officers were let in by the night guards. They stayed 81 minutes and left with 13 works. Nobody has been charged, and none of the art has come back.

The Gardner theft
What was takenVermeer’s The Concert, three works by Rembrandt, including his only seascape, Manet’s Chez Tortoni, five drawings by Degas, a work by Govert Flinck, a Chinese bronze beaker, and a bronze eagle that once topped a Napoleonic flagpole.
WorthThe FBI has estimated $200 million to $600 million. The Concert alone has been valued at about $250 million.
The reward$10 million, from the museum, for information that leads to the art.
Why the frames are emptyGardner’s will says the collection can’t be significantly changed. The frames stay on the walls, waiting.

9The Dutch RoomEmpty since 1990

Christ in the Storm on the Sea of Galilee

This is one of my favorite paintings in all of art history. A tiny boat, a wall of water, and light breaking through right where it needs to. It would be lovely to see it in person one day. Right now there’s only the frame.

It is Rembrandt’s only known seascape. He painted himself into the boat: the one man looking straight out at you. The thieves cut the canvas out of its frame.

The painting
Painted1633, oil on canvas, about 5 feet tall.
BoughtFor Isabella Stewart Gardner in 1898, through the art historian Bernard Berenson, for $6,000.
Where it hungThe Dutch Room, on the museum’s second floor.
Seen it?Call the museum’s tip line, listed on its theft page. The reward still stands.

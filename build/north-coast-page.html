The North Coast

Marin, Napa, Sonoma, and Mendocino · 2019, 2023, and 2024

North of the Golden Gate, California turns into redwoods, cold surf, and wine country. For thousands of years this was the land of the Coast Miwok, the Pomo peoples, and the Wappo. Then came, in quick order, Russian fur traders, the Gold Rush, the Army, and the lumber mills.

Most of what visitors love here was saved by someone: a grove, a beach, a stretch of coast. It is worth asking what each place was saved from, and who it was saved for. The photos here are the postcard. The small print under each one is the rest of the story.

Who the coast was saved for

The short version: who lived here, who arrived, and who got to decide what was worth keeping.

A short history of the North Coast
Thousands of yearsCoast Miwok, Kashaya and other Pomo peoples, and the Wappo live along this coast and in these valleys.
1812 to 1841The Russian-American Company builds Fort Ross on Kashaya Pomo land and brings Alaska Native hunters to take sea otters. By the late 1830s the otters are nearly gone, and the Russians sell out.
1848 and 1849Gold is found in the Sierra. Sam Brannan stocks stores with mining supplies, then walks through San Francisco waving a bottle of gold. His store at Sutter’s Fort takes in $150,000 a month.
1856 and 1857The government sets up the Mendocino Indian Reservation on the coast, and the Army builds a post to control it: Fort Bragg.
1859Brannan buys land at the top of Napa Valley and founds Calistoga as a hot springs resort.
1865 to 1869The reservation is closed. Many of its people are marched inland to Round Valley, and the coastal land is sold for $1.25 an acre.
1870sA homesteader finds stone logs near Calistoga and starts charging admission.
1885In St. Helena, an “Anti-Coolie League” tries to buy out the Chinatown whose workers dug the valley’s wine caves. The merchants take it to federal court and stay.
1906 to 1967After the earthquake, Fort Bragg pushes its trash over the cliffs into the sea.
1908William Kent gives Muir Woods to the nation to stop a water company from flooding it.
1913In Congress, Kent helps pass the law that lets San Francisco flood Hetch Hetchy in Yosemite.
1920s and 1930sMore than 100,000 tons of Goat Rock are blasted away for a jetty at the mouth of the Russian River. The jetty fails.
1972 and 1976California voters create the Coastal Commission, and the Coastal Act follows, with public access to the coast as one of its goals.
2020 to 2022Fort Bragg considers a new name. It keeps the old one.

1Muir Woods2023, afternoon

Looking up

Just north of the Golden Gate, and the only thing to do is tip your head back.

William Kent gave this grove to the nation in 1908 to keep a water company from flooding it, and named it for his friend John Muir. Five years later, in Congress, Kent helped pass the law that flooded Hetch Hetchy in Yosemite. It cost him Muir’s friendship.

Muir Woods quick facts
The treesCoast redwoods, the tallest trees on Earth.
ProtectedDeclared a national monument by President Theodore Roosevelt on January 9, 1908, using the Antiquities Act.
VisitingSince 2018, parking and shuttle seats have to be reserved ahead of time.

3St. Helena2024, midday

Home base in wine country

Where I stayed for the Napa Valley part of the trip, with vineyards right outside and a sky that looked painted on.

Chinese workers dug the wine caves at Beringer, right here in St. Helena, by hand. By the 1880s they were up to 80 percent of the valley’s vineyard labor. In 1885, 300 locals formed an “Anti-Coolie League” and tried to buy Chinatown out from under them. The merchants hired a lawyer and refused to move.

St. Helena quick facts
WhereNear the middle of Napa Valley, on Highway 29, between Napa and Calistoga.
The cavesThe Beringer caves run more than 1,200 feet, dug with picks and shovels.
RememberedA plaque now marks St. Helena’s old Chinatown.

4The Petrified Forest, Calistoga2024, late afternoon
1 of 5

Redwoods, turned to stone

A short loop through the woods, past trees that fell a few million years ago and never rotted.

The Wappo lived in this valley for thousands of years. By the time a homesteader started charging admission to see these logs in the 1870s, war and disease had nearly driven them out.

Petrified Forest quick facts
How it happenedA volcanic eruption about 3.4 million years ago knocked down a redwood forest and buried it in ash. Over time, silica soaked into the wood and turned it to stone.
The volcanoSeven miles northeast, in what is now Mount St. Helena.
Found byCharles Evans, known as “Petrified Charley,” around 1870.
Owned byOne family since 1914. It is a private park, not a state one.

5Goat Rock, Sonoma Coast2024, midday

Turn here

Yellow wildflowers, blue sky, and a sign pointing three good directions at once.

Goat Rock used to be joined to the shore. In the 1920s and ’30s, more than 100,000 tons of it were blasted off for a jetty to keep the Russian River’s mouth open for ships. The jetty failed.

Goat Rock quick facts
WhereSonoma Coast State Park, just south of where the Russian River meets the ocean at Jenner.
Look, don’t swimCold water, strong currents, and sneaker waves. Keep an eye on the ocean.
In the portfolioWhere the River Meets the Sea, and just down the coast, Edge of the Sonoma Coast at Duncan’s Landing.

7Glass Beach, Fort Bragg2019, late afternoon

Treasure, technically

Look down and the whole beach sparkles.

Fort Bragg began in 1857 as an Army post to control that reservation. It was named for Braxton Bragg, who later became a Confederate general. After 2020 the town talked about a new name. It kept the old one.

Glass Beach quick facts
Where the glass came fromAfter the 1906 earthquake, the town began dumping its trash over the cliffs here. It kept going until 1967, across three dump sites.
What the ocean didWaves broke the glass and pottery down and tumbled the pieces smooth.
ProtectedCalifornia State Parks took over the newest site in 2002, and it is now part of MacKerricher State Park.
Please leave itTaking glass is illegal and can bring a fine of up to $500.

7Two glass beachesFort Bragg and Kauaʻi
↔️Interactive photo. Drag the handle (or use the arrow keys) to compare Fort Bragg’s Glass Beach with the one on Kauaʻi.
↔
Fort Bragg, 2019Kauaʻi, 2022

Same story, two oceans

Twenty-four hundred miles apart, and both beaches are made of the same thing: what people threw away. Fort Bragg’s is mostly glass. Kauaʻi’s is mostly coral and shell, with the glass mixed in.

Both were town dumps. People stood on the cliffs and threw the trash into the sea, and a century of waves turned it into something people now drive hours to see.

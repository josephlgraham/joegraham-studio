Yosemite

People have lived in Yosemite Valley for thousands of years. The Ahwahneechee called it Ahwahnee. In 1864, in the middle of the Civil War, Abraham Lincoln signed the Yosemite Grant and set the valley aside for preservation and public use, the first time the U.S. government had ever done that. The idea behind every national park since started here. So did the question of who the public was.

Photographers were part of it from the beginning. Carleton Watkins’s pictures of the valley helped convince Congress, and decades later Ansel Adams made its cliffs and waterfalls famous around the world.

The photos here are the postcard. The small print under each one is the rest of the story. Tap a numbered pin on the map to jump to that stop.

Some backgroundBefore the stops

How Yosemite became Yosemite

The quick version: who lived here, who took charge, and who decided what the park was for.

A short history of the valley
Thousands of yearsThe Ahwahneechee live in the valley they call Ahwahnee. Miwok, Mono, and Paiute people travel and trade through the area.
1851The Mariposa Battalion, a state militia raised during the Mariposa War between Gold Rush settlers and Native people, attacks the Ahwahneechee in the valley and marches Chief Tenaya’s people toward a reservation. Many slip away and return.
June 30, 1864Abraham Lincoln signs the Yosemite Grant, setting the valley aside “for public use.” Ahwahneechee families are still living in it.
1868John Muir walks in from San Francisco and makes Yosemite his life’s cause.
1891 to 1913The U.S. Army runs the park. In 1903 the troopers on patrol are Buffalo Soldiers of the 9th Cavalry.
1913Over Muir’s years of protest, Congress lets San Francisco dam Hetch Hetchy, a second glacier valley inside the park, for city water.
1916The National Park Service is created. That same summer, a 14-year-old Ansel Adams makes his first trip to the valley.
1953 to 1969A new rule lets only year-round park employees live in the park. As Native families leave the valley’s Indian Village, their cabins are torn down or burned. The last family is gone by 1969.
2016 to 2019A hotel company that lost its contract claims it owns the trademarks to the park’s historic names. The Ahwahnee and Curry Village are renamed for three years, until a $12 million settlement brings the names back.
2020The Sierra Club, which Muir founded, publicly reckons with his derogatory remarks about Native and Black people.

1Arch Rock EntranceArriving

The way in

The west entrance is named for these two boulders. They lean together over the inbound lane, and you drive right under them.

Before there were rangers, the Army ran Yosemite. In 1903 the troopers patrolling it were Buffalo Soldiers of the 9th Cavalry.

Arch Rock quick facts
RoadHighway 140, called El Portal Road inside the park.
HistoryOpened July 31, 1926 as the “All-Year Highway,” the first road into Yosemite you could drive in every season. It turns 100 this year.
Why take itIt follows the Merced River canyon at low elevation, which makes it the easier winter route.
ElevationAbout 2,100 feet at the entrance.

2Tunnel ViewMorning

The first big look

The view everyone pulls over for: El Capitan on the left, Half Dome in the middle, Bridalveil Fall on the right.

“Yosemite” comes from a Miwok word usually translated as “they are killers.” It is what the neighbors called the people who lived here, and it is the name the soldiers kept.

Tunnel View quick facts
Built1933, together with the Wawona Tunnel right behind it.
The tunnel4,233 feet long, the longest highway tunnel in California. Workers bored it through solid granite in about two years.
Getting thereOn Wawona Road (Highway 41), at the east end of the tunnel. From Highway 140, it’s a short climb up Wawona Road.
In the portfolioMy favorite frame from this overlook, Cathedral of the High Sierra.
Ansel Adams was hereHis most famous Yosemite photograph, Clearing Winter Storm, was made from Inspiration Point, above this overlook. The trail up starts right here.
Historic statusThe tunnel and overlook were found eligible for the National Register of Historic Places in 1986. The overlook was restored and rededicated in 2008.

3Valley ViewLate in the day

The Merced holds still

A river-level view where the water slows down enough to hold a reflection of the whole scene.

Yosemite had a twin: Hetch Hetchy, another glacier valley with its own river. In 1913 Congress let San Francisco dam it for drinking water. It is still underwater.

Valley View quick facts
Also calledGates of the Valley.
What you seeEl Capitan, Cathedral Rocks, Sentinel Rock, and Bridalveil Fall, with the Merced in front.
WhereA small pullout on Northside Drive near the west end of the valley, by Pohono Bridge. Restroom, wheelchair accessible.
In the portfolioThe frame I kept from this spot, Mirror of the Merced.
Worth knowingIn the January 1997 flood, water stood 6 feet deep in this parking lot.

4Near El CapitanAfternoon

Gold against gray

California black oaks turn gold on the valley floor from late October into November. In the fall, acorns are a favorite food of the park’s bears.

These oaks were the valley’s pantry. Black oak acorns were a staple of the Ahwahneechee diet, and they burned the valley floor to keep the oaks healthy. When the burning stopped, the conifers crowded in.

Find the climbers

El Capitan rises about 3,000 feet straight up from the valley floor. From the road it looks like a wall of warm granite. See the little orange box? Tap to zoom in on it.

El Capitan quick facts
HeightAbout 3,000 feet from base to summit on its tallest face. The summit sits at 7,569 feet above sea level.
The nameGiven by the Mariposa Battalion in 1851, a loose Spanish rendering of the local Native name for the cliff. It means "the captain" or "the chief."
Climbing itMost parties take several days, sleeping on the wall. The fastest climbers do it in a couple of hours.
No ropesOn June 3, 2017, Alex Honnold climbed El Cap with no rope or safety gear at all, up the Freerider route in 3 hours and 56 minutes. The documentary about it, Free Solo, won the Oscar for best documentary.
Where to watchEl Capitan Meadow, along Northside Drive. Binoculars help a lot.
Watch liveYosemite Conservancy runs an El Capitan webcam from a dome near the Wawona Tunnel.
Seasonal closuresSome routes close each spring for nesting peregrine falcons, roughly March through mid-July.

Climbers have their own history of pushing back. In 1997 the park planned new dorms next to Camp 4, the climbers’ campground. Climbers sued, and in 2003 Camp 4 went on the National Register of Historic Places.

🔥El Cap’s other famous trick: the Firefall. For a couple of weeks each February, sunset lights up Horsetail Fall on El Cap’s east edge so it glows like lava for about ten minutes. Photographer Galen Rowell made the first known picture of it in 1973.

About the long lens

Most of this trip I shot with the small kit zoom on my Fujifilm X-T30 II. For this I switched to the XF70-300mm. At the long end, it takes a patch of wall that’s smaller than your thumbnail in the wide shot and fills the whole frame with it.

That’s the only reason these climbers show up at all. It’s one of my favorite finds of the whole trip.

5Yosemite FallsMidday

Where did the waterfall go?

Upper Yosemite Fall in late fall, down to a ribbon. The falls run on snowmelt, so what you see depends entirely on the season.

For almost a century the valley had a second, man-made waterfall: a bonfire pushed off Glacier Point on summer nights so hotel guests could watch the embers fall. The Park Service ended it in 1968.

Yosemite Falls quick facts
Total drop2,425 feet, the tallest waterfall in North America.
Three partsUpper fall 1,430 feet, middle cascades 675 feet, lower fall 320 feet.
When it flowsRoughly November through July, peaking in May. Often a trickle or dry by August. Fall storms can bring it back.
Easy walkA one-mile loop trail leads to the base of Lower Yosemite Fall.
MoonbowsOn clear spring nights near a full moon, moonlight makes a rainbow in the mist. John Muir wrote about them. To the eye it looks silver; a long exposure shows the color. How to see one.
📡See how much water is running right now. The park’s live Yosemite Falls webcam, run by Yosemite Conservancy.

On the trailAfternoon

Familiar, but not quite

Pinecones are everywhere where I live in Alabama. A loblolly cone fits in your palm. This one did not.

Sugar pine quick facts
Claim to fameThe tallest and most massive pine in the world, with the longest cones of any conifer.
Cone sizeUsually about 10 to 20 inches long, sometimes longer.
Record treeThe tallest sugar pine ever measured, about 274 feet, grows in Yosemite. It was found in 2015.
The nameThe sap has a sweet, sugary taste.

6The Ansel Adams GalleryYosemite Village

Standing in Ansel Adams’s footprints

You can’t point a camera at Yosemite without thinking about Ansel Adams. Almost every view in this feed, he got there first, in black and white, with a big view camera and a lot more patience than me.

Adams is remembered for landscapes with no people in them. In 1943 he drove over these mountains to Manzanar and photographed Japanese Americans held in the camp there. He called the book Born Free and Equal.

Ansel Adams and Yosemite
First visitJune 1916, at age 14. His parents gave him his first camera for the trip, a Kodak No. 1 Box Brownie.
Every year afterHe came back to Yosemite every year from 1916 until he died in 1984.
Early daysJoined the Sierra Club in 1919 and spent summers as caretaker of its LeConte Memorial Lodge in the valley. His first published photographs ran in the club’s 1922 Bulletin.
The breakthroughMonolith, the Face of Half Dome (1927), shot in fading light with a red filter to turn the sky nearly black.
The famous oneClearing Winter Storm, made from Inspiration Point above Tunnel View. Nobody is sure when. Adams himself gave both 1940 and 1944, and researchers have argued for dates as early as 1935.
Piano firstHe trained to be a concert pianist and practiced on the piano at Best’s Studio. He married the owner’s daughter, Virginia Best, there in 1928.
The Zone SystemDeveloped with Fred Archer: a way to plan exactly how light and dark every part of the final print will be, before you press the shutter.
Visit todayBest’s Studio, founded in 1902, is now The Ansel Adams Gallery in Yosemite Village, between the Visitor Center and the post office. Staff photographers lead camera walks, some to spots where Adams made his pictures. Check the gallery’s schedule.
🖼️See the man himself. Ansel Adams, Self-portrait, Monument Valley, Utah (1958): his shadow and tripod stretched across the desert floor. At the Amon Carter Museum of American Art.

7Valley campgroundNightfall

Home for the night

It looks cozier than it was. The coldest night I’ve ever spent.

Yosemite’s last Native residents lived in the valley’s Indian Village until 1969. As families moved out, the Park Service tore down or burned the empty cabins, some of them for firefighter training.

Camping in the valley
ElevationThe valley floor sits around 4,000 feet. Late fall nights can drop to freezing even after mild afternoons.
BookingValley campgrounds are reserved through Recreation.gov and fill up fast. Book as early as you can.
PackA sleeping bag rated colder than you think you need.
FoodFood, trash, and anything scented, even toothpaste and lip balm, go in the metal bear locker at your site.

Status updateEveryone asks

Bears seen: 0 🐻
Visits to Yosemite: 2

I didn’t see any. Plenty of people do. Here’s what the park wants you to know.

The grizzly is still on the state flag. The real ones didn’t make it: the last known grizzly in the region was shot in the early 1920s.

Yosemite bear facts
How manyRoughly 300 to 500 American black bears.
ColorMost aren’t black. They’re brown, blond, or reddish brown.
GrizzliesNone. The last known grizzly in the region was shot in the early 1920s. The species survives in California only on the state flag.
DistanceStay at least 50 yards away.
Bear sprayNot allowed in Yosemite.
DrivingSpeeding is one of the top killers of Yosemite bears. Slow down.
